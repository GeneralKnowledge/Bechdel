# Calloway

**Status:** First version, 15 Nov 2026. The city as it would be without the mystery. Canon places are in [canon/places.md](../canon/places.md).

A cathedral-sized provincial city on a slow river, about two hundred thousand people. Old town on the hill, the river and its moorings below, newer estates spreading east. It is proud of its Registry the way other cities are proud of a university, and slightly ashamed of how much of its money still comes from the Order's land.

## The council and the housing programme

Calloway has more people than homes. Delphine Teague chairs the council's housing committee and has staked her seat on **Ferrier Street: 340 homes, a third of them for rent at what people can pay**. The Order owns the land and has wanted to sell it for ten years, but a sale needs a vote of its chapter, and the chapter is split between those who want the money and those who think the Order should not become a property developer. Mother Hesketh can carry the vote.

The deal, in place before the book starts: Hesketh supports the sale in chapter; Delphine's family, which has opposed every move towards Senna's elevation for two hundred years on the grounds that the Teagues were robbed in 649, stops opposing the Cause. Neither woman would call it a deal. The opposition councillors call it one weekly in the *Courier*.

The housing vote is set for June. It is the first thing Delphine thinks about in the morning; Senna is the fourth or fifth.

## The Order as landlord

The Order is the second-largest landowner in the county and is bad at it. Recent embarrassments: it sold the old St Hild's school to a hotel group, which put a spa in the chapel; it has let three of its almshouses fall empty; its investment committee lost money on a shopping centre. Members of the Order argue about property far more than about doctrine, and the property arguments are the bitter ones.

## The Registry as an office

The Registry keeps the city's titles, as it has for nine hundred years, and is a civic department with a budget, a union and a backlog. Agnes Mure's great project is the **Copy Programme**: scanning every roll so that searches can be done on a screen. It is two years late and over budget, and she means to finish it before she retires. The search room charges by the hour; freelancers like Tamsin are admitted on a pass that can be withdrawn. The staff call the relic cases in the chapel "the window", because tourists press against them.

Agnes's worry, which is real and has nothing to do with Tamsin: confidence in title is what lets people buy houses. Every challenge to an old entry costs the Registry months.

## The faith as people argue about it

None of these has anything to do with the Cause. They are what members of the Keeping actually quarrel about in 1022.

- **The music.** The Naming Day hymn has been sung for two hundred years to an old tune called *Wallgate*, which everyone knows and nobody can sing in tune. For the anniversary the Order commissioned Juno to set it new. A petition to "keep *Wallgate*" has eleven hundred signatures and a letter in the *Courier* most weeks. Juno has kept *Wallgate*'s first four notes in her setting as a peace offering, and both sides hate that.
- **The two choirs.** The Registry Chapel choir and the choir of St Hild's on the Bridge have been rivals since anyone can remember. St Hild's was not asked to sing at the anniversary. Its director writes to the *Courier* about it under a series of thin pseudonyms. Several of Juno's anniversary sopranos sing in both and are spies for each side.
- **The short reading.** The Order has proposed that households may read their roll in a shorter form, naming the dead of the last two hundred years and then saying "and all our kept" for the rest. Large old households, whose Readings take two hours, are tempted. Aunt Ros calls it laziness with a blessing on it.
- **Screens.** Whether a household roll kept on a laptop or a phone "keeps". The Order says a roll must be written by hand to be read at the hearth, and a printout of a file does not count. Half of Calloway's younger households keep both and read from the paper one at the Reading out of politeness. Agnes's Copy Programme gets dragged into this argument by people who do not understand it.

## Small things

- Senna souvenirs for the 375th: mugs, a tea towel with the hymn on it (wrong second line), a "keep it twice" tote bag, a chocolate bar in the shape of a roll.
- People from the old town say "up the hill" and mean it as a class.
- Calloway thinks Wexham is slow, provincial and slightly holy. Wexham thinks Calloway is all mouth.
