Juno had called an extra rehearsal for the Wednesday before Christmas because the altos could not count, and so at seven o'clock the Registry Chapel was lit and unlocked and full of forty women in coats, and nobody was watching the door marked PRIVATE.

Tamsin came in from the corridor side. She had come up the hill by the staff entrance, round the back by the bins, where the reader still took her pass, and walked down the cream-and-green corridor past the fire extinguisher with its bow, in the half-dark, with the building's night smell of floor polish and paper. Nobody saw her. The night porter's radio was on in her cubby by the front. At the end of the corridor she put the pass to the chapel door, heard it click, and stood in the doorway at the back of the north aisle.

The choir was in the chancel, in two blocks, sopranos on the left. Juno stood in front of them on a box, in her rehearsal cardigan with the sleeves pushed up, beating time with a pencil. They were somewhere in the middle of the setting. It was not going well. Juno stopped them with both hands.

"Altos. It's four. It's been four since September. If you sing it in three, you will be on your own on Senna's Day in front of the Registrar-General and I will not come and get you."

Laughter. They went again.

The cases were along the north aisle, between Tamsin and the chancel, their lamps on low as they always were. In the evenings, with the main lights up for the choir, the glass caught the chancel and you had to stand close to see through your own reflection. Tamsin stood close.

She took her phone out of her coat and found the photograph. She had taken it on Sunday, at Bryony's kitchen table, while Bryony was filling the kettle: the margin of the letter of 668, the struck *xii*, the *xi*, and the stroke. She had taken six more since, at home, of the whole bundle, at the kitchen table under the anglepoise, with the curtains drawn. She had told herself that this was what you did with evidence you had been lent for a week. She had not told herself what it was evidence of.

She held the phone against the glass of the first case, beside the page with the four corrections.

It was the same. She had known it would be and it was still a shock, the way it is a shock to see your own handwriting on an envelope you do not remember sending. The angle was the same, a little steeper than a copybook's. The weight was the same, heavier at the head, as if the pen had been set down with a decision. And at the foot, the hook. Small, tight, turning back up on itself, and there, in the turn, the faint double line where the nib had lifted a hair and come down again: on the letter in her phone and on the roll under the glass, in the same place. Nobody who copied the stroke ever got that. Tamsin had drawn it herself a hundred times, at parties, on the backs of menus, and had never got it, because she had not known it was there until this moment.

She photographed the page through the glass. Then the next case, and the next, a correction in each, all the way down the aisle, the phone held flat to cut out the reflections of forty women in coats. The light was poor and she took each one twice. At the edge of the frame, between the second case and the third, the alms book lay open in its new case under its printed card, and came into three of the pictures without her meaning it to, a corner of cramped brown entries she did not stop to read.

Behind her the choir came to the last verse.

They had it now, or nearly. *Write her where the fire is kept*, low and steady, and Tamsin, with her phone flat against the glass, felt the four notes of *Wallgate* go through her and then fall away into Juno's line. *Read her when the house is still.* The third line hung, unresolved, on the long chord. *Though the hand that wrote her fail.* And then the altos brought it down, very quiet, *another hand will*, all forty of them barely singing, and the sopranos lifted *another will* out of it and handed it on to the roof.

Tamsin turned round.

Juno was looking straight at her down the length of the chapel. She must have seen her come in, or seen the phone, or seen something; she was looking at Tamsin over the heads of the sopranos with no expression at all, and her hands did not stop. They kept the beat through the last chord and held it, and let it go, and the choir let it go with her, and the sound went up into the dark of the roof and was gone.

"Better," Juno said, to the altos, not turning her head. "Much. Again from the third line."

Tamsin walked to the east end, away from the choir, into the side chapel where the tomb was.

It stood in the middle of the side chapel under its own low lamp, a stone chest on four squat feet, with the effigy on its lid worn smooth at the hands and face by two hundred years of people touching it. On the wall behind it was Carrow's tomb, smaller, set into the plaster: *Ysolde Carrow, Keeper, who died in office in 651, in honour.* People did not touch that one.

The inscription ran round the edge of the lid in letters cut deep and blacked in. Tamsin had read it once as a novice, on the day she was sworn, and never since, because it was the kind of thing you stopped reading.

*HERE LIES SENNA ALDOUS, REGISTRAR, WHO DIED FOR THE ROLLS. BROUGHT HERE FROM THE FIELD OUTSIDE THE WALLS IN THE KEEPERSHIP OF ORIEL VANCE, OUT OF THE GRAVE SIGNED WITH HER HAND.*

The grave signed with her hand. She stood and read it twice. She had always taken it as a figure of speech, if she had taken it as anything: the grave was known by her, sanctified, marked as hers. Now, with the phone in her pocket and the hook still in front of her eyes, she read it as a registrar would, literally. Someone in about 800 had gone out to the field of the unwritten and found a grave with the stroke on it, and had known what the stroke meant, and had dug there.

That was what it said. It did not say what she wanted it to say, which was something about 668. It was a dead saint's hand on a dead saint's grave, which was exactly where it ought to be. The hand in her phone was the one that was in the wrong place.

She thought: the same hand, twenty-one years later. Then: the same hand. Then, because she had been trained to say the next thing even when she did not like it: a hand cannot write twenty-one years after its owner is dead. So the owner was not dead. Or the hand was not the owner's. Or the date was wrong, or the letter was, or she was.

She did not think she was wrong. She had stood at the glass with the phone and the hook had been the hook. She felt, standing there in the side chapel with the choir going again behind her, more certain than she had felt about anything since the morning she had cleared her desk, and at the Registry, for fifteen years, being certain and being right had been the same thing.

The choir finished at half past eight. Tamsin was waiting in the north porch when Juno came out with her bag and her folder and her keys, with the last of the altos going off down the hill in twos.

"You came," Juno said.

"I was passing."

"You were in the chapel. With your phone." Juno locked the porch door with the choir key, which she was not supposed to have overnight and always did. "Agnes doesn't let freelancers in after hours."

"I used my old pass."

"I thought you gave that back."

"I meant to."

Juno put the keys in her coat pocket and looked at her, in the light from the porch lamp, the way she had looked at her down the chapel over the heads of the sopranos. Then she looked away, down the hill, at the lights of the city.

"What were you photographing?"

"Something for the orchard," Tamsin said. "A correction on one of the eastern rolls. I wanted to see the original."

It was not untrue. It was so nearly true that she could hear, in the gap between it and the rest, how much it was leaving out, and she thought Juno could hear it too. But Juno only nodded, and took her arm, because the steps were icy, and they went down the hill together towards the bus station, talking about the altos.
