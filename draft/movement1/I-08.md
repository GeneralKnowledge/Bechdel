By nine o'clock there were fourteen of them in Aunt Ros's front room and two of the chairs had come from next door. Kate had counted coats on the banister on her way in, because counting was what she did when she walked into a room, and had counted again when Tamsin and Juno arrived, late, with a bottle and a tin of the shortbread Juno's mother made every year and nobody liked.

Tamsin had a cold face from the bus and the look of someone who had been somewhere else all week. She kissed Kate's mother and Aunt Ros, and said the right things about the fire, and then sat on the arm of the sofa by the door, which was where people sat who were planning to leave early, or who did not want to be asked to read.

"She's not eaten," Juno said, quietly, taking off her scarf.

"Since when?"

"Since about Tuesday, I think, in any serious way."

Kate looked. Tamsin was holding her coat across her knees as if she might be asked for it at the door. Her hands were folded on top of it, and the right thumb was moving over the side of the second finger, over the callus, back and forth. Kate had seen patients do that the night before an operation, the ones who said they were not worried.

The pie was in the oven. Aunt Ros had put the roll on the little table by the hearth, on the green cloth, with the reading lamp angled over it, and beside the roll the brown box that held the family papers, which came out once a year for this and otherwise lived under her bed with the photograph albums and a bag of wool she had been going to do something with since 1998.

"Katherine," said Aunt Ros, who was the only person alive who called her that. "You're doing Marit."

"I did Marit last year."

"You do her best."

That was a lie, and a kind one, and also Ros's way of saying her knees were bad. Kate took the box on to her lap and found the diary. It was not the real diary, which was in the archive's care at the Corn Exchange and could be visited by appointment, but the copy Ros's mother had made by hand in the sixties, in a school exercise book, with the dates underlined in red.

There was a ritual to this too, though nobody had ever written it down. Someone read from Marit before the roll, a few entries, the same few, because Marit had kept the household after Senna died, and because the diary was the only thing in the box that made anybody laugh.

Kate read the November entries, the madder for the Pryor girls and the tooth that Wren wanted written in the roll. Her cousin Bea's youngest, who was nine and had lost a tooth on Christmas Eve, looked round at her mother with an expression of total vindication, and the room laughed, and Kate read on, because the laugh was the point. She read the rain, and the yard that was a pond with a business attached. She read the spoiled madder that Marit gave away to the woman on the chapel porch, three lengths, the brown end of the batch; that she knew the colour by the smell before it was unrolled, and said it would do for a blanket if nobody looked. *Nobody does.*

Then she read the winter, and Tobi's cough, and the honey and the kettle, which got a murmur because Tobi was everyone's grandmother a dozen times over and the cough had evidently not killed her. Then she read the last one. *S. tired and secret all winter. I have stopped asking. W. says she talks in her sleep, and it is numbers.*

She closed the exercise book.

"Working all hours for the rolls," said Kate's mother, to the room, as she said every year. "And they never thanked her."

"They have now," said someone by the window, and somebody else said, "Well, they will at midsummer," and there was a little ripple of pride and embarrassment, the way there always was in that family when the Cause came up, as if they had all been caught standing too near a famous relative at a wedding.

"I always think she sounds furious," Kate said.

Aunt Ros looked at her over her glasses. "Who?"

"Marit. *I have stopped asking.* She's furious. Senna's out every night, she's got two children coughing, she's running the yard for her mother, and she's stopped asking. That's not a woman who's proud of anybody."

"She's a woman who's tired," said Ros. "So would you be."

"I am. I'm furious too."

There was laughter at that, because Kate had come off three nights the morning before, and everyone knew it.

"It's after that I mean, anyway," Kate said. "After Senna dies. She never writes about her again. Not once in thirty years. You'd think there'd be something. A date. Her name at the Reading."

"She had two children to bring up who'd lost their mother," Ros said. "You don't write that down. You keep it small, so they can carry it. That's not anger. That's what you do." She said it lightly, and she meant it entirely. Ros had been left on her own at thirty-one with a girl of six to bring up, and she had never once, in Kate's hearing, talked about it at a Reading.

"You can be angry and do that," Kate said. "They're not opposites."

"Nobody said they were opposites."

"You said it wasn't anger."

"I said it was what you do."

"She was cross with everybody until she was seventy-eight," said Bea, from the floor, with her daughter's head on her knee. "Mum's got the bit where she falls out with the woman from the bakery for nine years."

"Over a loaf," said Kate's mother.

"Over a principle," said Ros, "that happened to be a loaf."

The timer went in the kitchen. Ros began to get up and four people told her to sit down, and Kate's mother went instead, and the smell of the pie came down the hall ahead of her, and nobody said any more about Marit. They never did. It was the same argument every year, give or take, and Kate sometimes thought that the family kept it the way it kept the shortbread.

Ros put on her reading glasses and drew the lamp down over the roll, and the room went quiet in the way it did, all at once, as if somebody had shut a door.

The Aldous roll began in 588. It was not the original, which was in the Registry like every household's oldest roll, but the family's own fair copy, made in Ros's grandmother's time, with a new sheet stitched on whenever someone was born or named or taken in. The first names were short and strange, and Ros read them in her archive voice, carefully, because she was the only one in the room who could, and after each the room answered *kept*.

Kate had answered *kept* every year of her life. She had answered it as a child who did not know what it meant and as a girl who thought it was embarrassing, and at twenty-three in a ward kitchen on a night shift, alone, at midnight, with a cup of tea, because her own patient had died at eleven and she could not face going home to read. She did not perform it now and did not think about it. It was like answering a register. But she listened, as she always did, to who said it with her.

Her mother said it firmly, like signing for a parcel. Bea said it with her eyes closed. Bea's nine-year-old said it slightly ahead of everyone, because she was competitive. Juno said it in tune, which Kate found unaccountably moving, as if it were a note she had been given. And Tamsin, on the arm of the sofa by the door, said it some of the time and some of the time only moved her mouth, and Kate noticed which names she let go by.

The centuries went past by the hearth. Ros's voice had the slight drop at the end of each name that meant *and here is the next*, and the answers came back steady, fourteen voices, then thirteen, as Bea's youngest fell asleep.

"Senna Aldous," Ros read, in her place among the dead of 647, in the same voice as the rest.

"Kept," said the room.

Tamsin's voice caught on it. It was not much: a breath taken in the wrong place, the word coming half a beat late and too low, as if it had snagged on something on the way up. Juno looked round at her. Nobody else did. Ros went on to Marit's mother, and the room answered, and Tamsin's hands on her coat had gone very still.

It was such a small thing that Kate nearly let it go. But she knew that voice. She had heard Tamsin say *kept* at fourteen, at the Reading in Tamsin's own mother's house, sullenly, and at twenty-two, newly sworn, with a kind of private astonishment, as if she had been handed something heavy and found she could carry it. She had not heard it in years. Tamsin had stopped coming to Readings, and stopped going to chapel, and had stopped, as far as Kate could tell, believing anything she could not look up; and Kate had decided some time ago not to mind.

She minded now. She found she was glad.

They came to the eighteen hundreds, which took a while, because the Aldouses had been many and poor and had named everyone after everyone else. And then, near the end of the roll, a name with no surname and a different kind of entry beneath it.

"Nell, found on the Infirmary step in the winter of 1890, and taken in at this hearth," Ros read. "Kept."

"Kept."

Juno leaned across. "What was that?"

"Taking in," Kate whispered. "When a household writes someone in who was never written. A foundling, usually. Somebody who died in your house with no one to read her." She thought about how to say it to someone who had grown up in a household too small for it ever to have happened. "No keeper. No oath. Just the household. You say she's yours, and then she is."

"Who was Nell?"

"Nobody knows. That's the point of her."

Juno sat back and looked at the roll for a long moment, and then at Tamsin, who was looking at nothing.

Then it was 1940, and 1971, and Kate's grandmother, and the room answered *kept* for her more loudly than for anyone, and Kate's mother put a hand over her eyes for a second, and then it was over and the lamp went up and everyone stood at once, the way they always did, and went in search of plates.

Kate found Tamsin in the hall with her coat already on. Juno was in the kitchen being shown the pie dish that had belonged to Ros's mother.

"You haven't eaten."

"I'll eat at home."

"You won't." Kate leaned on the banister among the coats. She had been carrying the question round all evening like a bottle she had been asked to bring. The committee had asked her in November, at the end of a meeting about chairs, whether she might mention it to her friend, the one who had been at the Registry. Kate had said she would see. "Listen. You know the Cause has a lay committee. I'm on it."

"I know."

"They need someone who can read old hands. Properly. Not the Order's people, someone who'll check things and say if they're wrong, so it can't come back on us. Hesketh's lot are very nice but they're all believers, and the Examiner's an Examiner, she's paid to say no." She was saying it badly. She had meant to say it well. "It's the anniversary. It's Senna. It'd be something for you to come back to that wasn't the Registry and wasn't Agnes. And I'd like it."

Tamsin did not answer. She looked at the hall floor, at the tiles Ros had been meaning to have regrouted since Kate was at school, and her hands were in her coat pockets, pushed down, as if they were cold.

Kate had seen enough people not answer to know there were many kinds, and she had heard this one in the front room twenty minutes ago.

"You don't have to say now," Kate said, more gently. "Think about it. Happy new year, you idiot."

"Happy new year."

Juno came out of the kitchen with two foil parcels of pie, one for each of them, and Kate kissed them both, and watched them down the path into the dark, Juno talking, Tamsin with her head down and her hands still in her pockets. Then she went back in to help with the plates, and to tell Ros that the committee would be thrilled.
