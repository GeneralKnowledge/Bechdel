The chapter house had been built for a hundred keepers and was used, most months, by eleven, so that every sitting of anything in it had the air of a play performed to an audience that had mostly left. Perpetua took the Examiner's chair, which was on the left of the dais, lower than the Advocate's on the right, and older, and harder, and had been designed by someone who believed doubt should be uncomfortable. She arranged her papers. She arranged her knees.

"Sister Examiner," said the clerk of the chapter.

"Present."

"Mother Advocate."

"Present," said Hesketh, from the other chair, and gave Perpetua across the dais the look she had been giving her at the start of sittings for thirty years, which meant *here we go*, and to which the correct reply was nothing at all.

There were more people than usual. The anniversary had brought them. Twenty or so in the body of the house, keepers and lay members, the lay committee in a row near the front with their coats on their knees; three in the press seats, one of whom was Fen Archer of the *Courier*, writing already, though nothing had happened; and in the gallery, alone, upright, in a camel coat, Councillor Delphine Teague.

Perpetua noticed her the way she noticed a signature in an unexpected hand at the foot of a charter. The Teagues did not come to sittings of the Cause. For two hundred years they had sent letters, about once a generation, to say that the family's position was unchanged, the family's position being that the Teagues had been robbed in 649 and that it ill became the Order to canonise the clerk who had robbed them. Delphine's grandmother had sent the last one. It was in Perpetua's files, filed under *Objections, standing*.

Well, it was the anniversary, and Delphine was a councillor. Councillors came to things. Perpetua made a note of it and turned to the business.

The business was procedure, as it always was at the opening of a Cause, and procedure was a thing Perpetua loved without reservation, the way some people love bell-ringing. The Advocate presented the petition for the elevation of Senna Aldous, registrar, of this city. The chapter received it. The Examiner entered her formal doubts. Perpetua had written them in September and had read them aloud in June and in March before that, as Examiner of the preliminary inquiry, and they had not changed, because there had been nothing to change them: that the life of the candidate rested principally on a single text written a century and a half after her death; that the relics had not been examined by any method later than the Order's own of 649; that the tomb had not been opened since the translation of about 800; and that the candidate's death was attested in no contemporary document that the Examiner had been able to find.

She read them in her examining voice, which was dry and quick, and heard the lay committee shift in their seats as she always did when she got to *no contemporary document*. Hesketh did not shift. Hesketh had her hands folded on the Advocate's desk and was looking at the far wall with an expression of perfect courtesy, and Perpetua knew she was composing her reply to the third doubt, the one about the tomb, and that it would be good.

It was. Hesketh stood, and said that the Advocate welcomed the Examiner's doubts as the Order had always welcomed doubt, as a kind of love; that the relics had been examined many times by the only method that mattered, which was the faith of those who stood before them; and that if the chapter wished it, the Advocate would ask for the tomb to be opened at the translation in April, before witnesses, so that the Examiner could see for herself what generations had seen without looking.

There was a murmur at that. Fen Archer wrote faster.

Then the chapter set the timetable. Hearings in the spring, the translation of the bones to the new shrine on Senna's Day, the ninth of April, and the vote at midsummer chapter. The clerk read the dates aloud and the chapter said *aye*, and it was done, and the Cause of Senna Aldous was open in the anniversary year, and nobody clapped, because nobody ever did.

"And now," said the clerk, "Honor Bale, who will read for us on Senna's Day, has asked to rehearse the death, so that the chapter may hear it in this room."

Honor came up from the body of the house on a stick, eighty-one and very small, and was helped to the lectern by a lay member, and put the stick against it and her reading copy on it, a battered book with paper slips sticking out of it like feathers. She did not open it. She had not needed to open it for twenty years. She put one hand flat on the cover and lifted her chin, and Perpetua, who had heard her every Senna's Day since she was a novice, felt the room change around her as a room does when someone who knows how to read aloud is about to do it.

"*Now in the spring of the year six hundred and forty-seven,*" Honor said, "*there came against the Registry of Calloway certain of a great house.*"

She had a voice like an old instrument, worn thin in the middle and still true at both ends. She took the mob up from the river and the lamp and the gate, and she put Senna's back against the door and her hand on the bolt, and the room was so quiet that Perpetua could hear Fen's pencil stop. *They struck it until the lamp went out.* The rolls went out one by one by the chapel way. The door gave.

"*She said, There is nothing here of yours. And she said, Write me where the fire is kept.*" Honor paused, as she always did. "*And her hand was on the bolt, and her hand was steady. She asked no one's name, for she knew them all.*"

Perpetua's pen, which had been resting, moved a little on the page. She did not know why. Something in the rhythm; a pair of sentences that sat very slightly differently from the sentences round them, the way a later hand sits on a page even when it has learnt the earlier one perfectly. She waited for it to come clear and it did not, and Honor went on, *And she died for the rolls*, to the end, and the room let its breath out, and then, against all custom, somebody in the lay committee clapped, twice, and stopped, mortified.

Perpetua looked up at the gallery. Delphine Teague had stood to leave, and on her way to the stair she looked down at the dais, at the Advocate's chair, and Hesketh, who was gathering her papers, looked up, and the two women nodded to each other. It was a very small nod. It was the nod of two people who have already had the conversation and do not need to have it again in public.

Perpetua filed that too.

---

Lunch was in the chapter refectory, because it was a sitting day and Hesketh said it was quicker, which was true, and because Hesketh liked the refectory's soup, which was indefensible.

"Your third doubt," Hesketh said, "was beneath you."

"My third doubt is the only one with a remedy. You can't examine a Great Life. You can open a tomb."

"I offered to."

"You offered to in front of Fen Archer, so that if I said no I would look frightened of a skeleton." Perpetua broke her bread roll, which was stale. "It was very elegant. I shall say yes, of course."

"Of course you will."

"And when they lift the lid, Hesketh, and find whatever they find, you will have staked the Cause on a box of bones that nobody has looked at in two hundred years."

"I'll have staked it on Senna." Hesketh ate her soup. She looked tired, Perpetua thought; tired in the skin under the eyes, the way she had looked the winter she had the trouble with her heart and pretended it was a cold. Her tablets were beside her plate, in the little enamel box Perpetua had given her one birthday as a joke and which she now used without irony. "Which is where it's always been staked."

"It's staked on Oriel Vance, and you know it, and you're a historian."

"I'm a historian who believes. You're a palaeographer who doesn't. We've been having this lunch for thirty years."

"The lunches were better when we were young."

"The soup wasn't."

Perpetua laughed, which she had not meant to do, and Hesketh looked pleased with herself, and for a while they talked about other things: the chapter vote in June on the Ferrier Street land, which Hesketh was going to carry and was tired of carrying; Honor's eyes, which were going; a novice who had misdated a charter by a century and been, Perpetua thought, very nearly right for the wrong reason.

"The Teague woman was in the gallery," Perpetua said, when the coffee came.

"Delphine. Yes."

"I don't remember a Teague at a sitting of the Cause before."

"It's the anniversary," Hesketh said, and stirred her coffee, and did not look up. "Everyone's coming to everything."

Perpetua let it go. She paid, because it was her turn. On the steps outside, in the cold, Hesketh put a gloved hand on her arm for a moment, to steady herself on the ice or for no reason, and said, "You'll be fair to her, won't you?" and Perpetua said she was always fair, it was her job, and did not say that she had sat through the opening of four Causes as Examiner and never yet, until this one, found herself hoping to lose.
