Orla Rook arrived at the orchard gate in a pharmacist's tunic under a good wool coat, with her car keys still in her hand, and said she had forty minutes. Her sister was already in the orchard. Tamsin could see her at the bottom of the slope by the brook, a figure in a green fleece doing something to a tree with her back to the lane, and she did not look up when the car door shut.

"She knows you're here," Orla said. "She's making a point."

"That's all right. I'll start at the top."

"Start wherever you like. You'll end up in the same place." Orla held out a hand. Her hands were very clean, the nails short, a plaster on one thumb, and she shook like someone who had shaken a great many hands across a counter and did not expect to remember any of them. "You had the papers from Gilmour's?"

"I've got the Registry extract and both your mother's papers. I'll want to see the stones."

"There are stones," Orla said, as if that settled something, and opened the gate.

The orchard ran down from the lane to the brook, four acres, the ground still white in the hollows from the night's frost and wet everywhere else. At the top, near the lane, the trees were young and planted in rows a machine could get between. Halfway down the slope they changed. A line of old pear trees crossed the orchard from side to side, thirty of them, thick in the trunk and leaning downhill, their bark split and grey-green with lichen, and below them the trees were old too, apples mostly, in no rows at all. The upper parcel had been farmed; the lower had been kept.

Tamsin had the extract in a plastic sleeve. It was a photocopy of the Registry plan with the parcels marked, and down the middle of the pear row, as clearly as a ruler could put it, ran the line between the two titles. The upper parcel went to Orla and the lower to Bryony, and the row was cut in half lengthways like a loaf.

She found the first stone by the lane, in the hedge bottom where the plan said it would be, a squared block worn round at the top with a groove cut in it. She knelt on her folded coat and scraped the moss off the groove with her thumbnail and photographed it with the tape laid across, and Orla watched from the path with her arms folded and said nothing, which Tamsin took as approval.

"Your mother left the two parcels separately," Tamsin said, standing up.

"Our mother thought she was being fair."

"And the line between them is the Registry line."

"The Registry line is in the middle of the pear row." Orla said it with a pharmacist's precision, as if reading a dose. "Which is nonsense. Nobody has ever farmed it like that. The row has always gone with the top. You go along the west side of it, there's a ditch, you can see where it used to be. That's the line."

"Who told you that?"

"Everyone who ever farmed it."

Tamsin wrote it down. *O.: west side of row, old ditch. Source: usage.* She was aware of the green fleece at the bottom of the hill, still turned away, still doing something careful to a branch with a pair of loppers.

"I've got a buyer," Orla said. "I expect Gilmour's told you. She's a builder, she wants to put two houses on the lane frontage. She needs the pear row for the access, the turning space. Without it the site's worth half. With it I can pay for my daughter's next three years and not lie awake. She's given me till the end of May to show her a clean title." She looked at her watch. "So. I need you to tell me the row's mine. Or tell me it isn't, and then I'll know."

"I'll tell you what the record shows."

"That's what I said."

It was not quite, but Tamsin let it go. She walked down to the pear row. The trees were older than anything else in the valley that was still alive, and close to, they were in poor shape and remarkable: hollow in two cases, propped in one, and pruned recently, hard and correctly, with the cuts sealed. She stood in the middle of the row and looked along it, east, then west. There was the trace of a ditch on the west side, as Orla had said, a soft line in the grass. On the east side there was nothing but a slight change in the slope where the apples began.

"Bryony," Orla called, not loudly. "She's here."

The figure at the bottom straightened, considered the tree for a moment longer, and then came up the hill without hurrying. She was smaller than her sister and broader, in boots caked to the ankle, and she had a pruning saw hooked through her belt and a cut across the back of one hand that she had not bothered to cover. She nodded to Tamsin. She did not look at Orla at all.

"You'll want the stones," she said.

"I've seen the one by the lane."

"There's one at the brook. And one in the row." Bryony walked off along the pear row and Tamsin followed, and after a moment, so did Orla, three women in single file along a line of trees, like a procession that had forgotten what it was for.

The stone in the row was half buried under the roots of the eleventh tree, and Bryony knelt and cleared it with her hands, quickly, as if she had done it before. It was older than the lane stone, rougher, and its groove ran north to south, not along the row but across it.

"That's not a parcel stone," Tamsin said.

"No."

"What is it?"

"It's been there longer than any of them." Bryony sat back on her heels. "Mum said it marked the old line. Before the Registry drew theirs. The row goes with the bottom field, to the far side of the trees. That side." She pointed east, to where the slope changed. "As it was always worked."

"Can you tell her," Orla said, to Tamsin, "that a stone under a tree is not a title?"

"Can you tell her," Bryony said, also to Tamsin, "that a ditch isn't either?"

Tamsin said, "Neither of them is," which was true and pleased nobody.

They walked the rest of it like that. At the brook there was the third stone, tilted, and Bryony's account of a flood in her grandmother's time. At the western hedge there was Orla's account of a fence their mother had taken down. Each sister told Tamsin a thing and the other sister answered it, through her, in the third person, as if Tamsin were an interpreter or a wall with a hole in it. Tamsin had seen a lot of this. It was nearly always money, in her experience, or the house, or who had sat by whose bed at the end, which was money by another route. She guessed it was money here, because one sister had a buyer and one had no coat on in December and both of them kept looking at the trees.

At the bottom of the orchard there was a low brick shed with a door half open, and inside it, along the wall, a row of boots. Most were Bryony's sort, cracked and enormous. One pair, at the end, was clean, smaller, and had not been worn in a long time. Neither sister looked at them, and Tamsin noted that they did not look, and did not ask.

At the gate Orla looked at her watch again. She had been gone fifty minutes.

"What do you charge?"

"Three hundred and fifty a day. I'd think eight days, with the Registry searches."

"Three hundred," said Orla.

Tamsin had been expecting that, and had built it in. "Three twenty. Split between you, so it comes out of both parcels."

Orla looked at Bryony for the first time that morning, a look that went past her sister's shoulder rather than at her face, and Bryony said, "Fine," to the hedge, and that was the contract.

"When will we know?"

"By the new year. I'll have an interim view sooner, if I find anything early."

"Will you find anything?"

"There's always something," Tamsin said. "Rolls are written by people. People get things wrong, or get them right for reasons nobody wrote down. Usually it's one of those."

"And which do you think this is?"

"I think it's eight days' work."

Orla almost smiled, and got into her car, and wound the window down. "You were at the Registry, weren't you? Gilmour's said. Proper job."

"For fifteen years."

"Why did you leave?"

"I wanted to work for myself," Tamsin said, which was the sentence she used, and which had stopped feeling like a lie because she had said it so often.

Orla nodded, as if she had heard a great many reasons across a counter and believed perhaps one in three, and drove off up the lane towards the high street and the pharmacy, where someone was presumably waiting for her.

Bryony was still at the gate.

"She'll sell it," she said, to Tamsin, though there was nobody else now to say it through. "She'll sell the trees for a turning circle. Two houses. They're not even nice houses. I've seen the drawings."

"I'm only doing the title."

"I know what you're doing." Bryony looked at her for a moment, and Tamsin had the sense of being measured, not unkindly, as a gate is measured before it is hung. "Mum's got papers. Old ones. In a box. I'll have a look."

"If there's anything about the boundary, I'd like to see it."

"I said I'd have a look."

She went back down the orchard to her tree and the loppers, and did not turn round. The bus back to Calloway went from the market cross at ten past twelve. Tamsin walked up Pell Lane with the plastic sleeve under her coat to keep it dry and worked out, on the way, that three twenty for eight days was two thousand five hundred and sixty pounds, less what she owed, which left her about nine hundred, and that she would tell Juno it was nearer four.
