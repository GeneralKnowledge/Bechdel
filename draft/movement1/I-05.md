Bryony had cleared the kitchen table for it, which Tamsin understood was a concession. Everything that usually lived there had gone to the dresser in a heap: seed catalogues, a bowl of pears going soft at the shoulders, a tin of hand cream with its lid off, and the solicitor's letters, still in their envelopes. The table had been wiped and was drying in long arcs.

"Sit there," Bryony said, and pointed at the chair with its back to the window, so that the light would fall on the paper and Tamsin's own face would be against it. Tamsin did not think she had meant the second part.

The box was on the floor by Bryony's boots. Tamsin had expected a deed box, black tin with a family name painted on it. It was a sewing box, walnut, with a split in the lid mended with brass tacks. Bryony lifted out a bundle tied with tape that had once been green, put it on the table between them, sat down, and laid her hands flat on either side of it, as if it might try to leave.

"That's them."

"May I?"

"That's what you're here for."

Tamsin took her cotton gloves out of her bag. Bryony watched her put them on with the look of someone watching a doctor wash her hands, faintly insulted on the paper's behalf.

"They've been handled," Bryony said. "My mother read them. Her mother."

"Then they've done well."

The tape had been tied and untied so often that it had a permanent kink where the knot went. Inside, the letters were folded in three, each with a date written small in the top corner of the outer fold, the way people kept correspondence before anybody had a filing cabinet: 647, 648, 651. The paper was rag, soft, gone the colour of weak tea at the edges. Somebody had once sorted them into order, and somebody later had put two of them back wrong. There were thirty or so, and a few more folded inside others.

She read the first because it was first. It was short. It was written to someone called Lise, and it was about a hard frost and a child who could not hold a pen for chilblains, and the writer's opinion that chilblains were a failing of the school stove and not of the child. It was signed *Senna*.

That was nothing. There had been three Sennas in Tamsin's year at school, and a Senna on the night desk at the Infirmary who had once told Kate that she would kill the next person who asked her if she was named for the saint, which she was.

"Which one is the orchard?"

"Look for it," Bryony said. "You're the one charging."

That was fair. Tamsin unfolded the letters one at a time, read the date and the first line of each, and laid them in rows on the table, careful of the damp. Most were to Lise. One was to a carter about a debt, and one to a child's mother, and then it was Lise again. The hand did not change much over thirty years. It got smaller, if anything, and the lines closer, as if paper had become dearer, or the writer had more to say and less time. It was a clerk's hand. Tamsin had been looking at clerks' hands since she was twenty-two and knew one the way a farrier knows a horse that has been shod by a good smith: by what was not wrong with it.

Bryony got up and filled the kettle and did not switch it on.

The letter of 668 was folded inside one of 667, which was why it had taken her so long. It began with the date written out in full, *The 9th of May, 668*, and went straight to business, the way the best of them did. *The Tench girl came about the lower field and I have let it to her for the summer on your terms, not hers.* The Tench girl was to graze it *from the brook to the pear row and the pear row with it, to its eastern edge, as your mother worked it.*

Tamsin read it twice and then looked up through the window, past Bryony's shoulder, at the pear row itself, thirty trees black and wet in the December light, the old ones leaning downhill as if they were listening for the brook.

"Well?" Bryony said.

"It says what your family's always said. The whole row goes with the lower field."

"I told you."

"The roll doesn't say that. The roll puts the line down the middle."

"The roll's wrong."

"Rolls are wrong all the time. That isn't the same as being able to show it." She heard herself and tried again, because Bryony's face had closed. "It helps. A letter from the household, describing how the land was worked, three hundred and fifty years ago: it helps. It describes use. A court will want to know whether it describes title." She looked at the line again. *As your mother worked it.* People had always worked land in one shape and registered it in another; half her income came from the difference. This was the Rooks' own story about their trees, set down by somebody who lived in the house and had heard it at the table, and the roll had been right, probably, all along. She was already composing the paragraph for the report.

Then she looked at the margin.

Beside the rent, *eleven shillings*, someone had written *xii*, struck it through and put *xi* after it. The figure did not interest her. The stroke did. It came down at a slant, very slightly heavier at the start, as if the pen had been set down with a decision, and at the bottom it turned back on itself in a small hook, tight, like a thread caught on a nail.

She knew it the way she knew the steps up to the Registry. For fifteen years she had walked through the Registry Chapel every working morning, past the cases where the relic rolls lay open under their low lamps, and in the case nearest the door, where the light was best, there was a page with four corrections on it and that same hook at the foot of each. Every registrar in Calloway could draw it. People drew it on napkins at Registry parties; it was the thing you did when you had been sworn and had had a glass of wine. Tamsin had drawn it herself. It had never looked like this, because drawn is not the same as made. This one had the pressure in the right place, at the turn, where the nib had been lifted a hair and set down again, and nobody copying it ever got that, because nobody copying it knew it was there.

She looked at the date. It said 668.

She looked at the stroke again, and then, carefully, at the next letter, and found it there too, in the margin of 670, beside a word misspelt and put right. And in 655. And in 649.

The kettle clicked. Bryony had switched it on after all.

"Tea?"

"Please." Her voice was all right. "No sugar."

"I know. You said last time."

Tamsin took off her glasses, which she had not needed, and put them on top of her head, and laid the letter of 668 square to the edge of the table, as though its position mattered. The saint died in 647. That was not a fact that needed checking. It was on the tomb, on the tea towels, on the front of the *Courier* every Saturday this month. Senna Aldous had died for the rolls in the spring of 647, in the thirty-first year of her age, and the hand on the rolls in the chapel was hers because it could not be anybody else's.

A pupil, then. A clerk who had learnt from her. A woman who admired the saint and had taught herself the stroke, as Tamsin had, but better; who had come to Wexham with it and kept it all her life. That was possible. Hands were taught. She told herself it was possible, and she noticed that she was telling herself.

"Is something wrong with it?"

"No," Tamsin said. "It's in very good condition. Someone's kept it dry."

"The box was my great-grandmother's." Bryony put a mug down well away from the paper. "Mum used to say they were Grandmother Senna's. Not my grandmother. Somebody's."

"Who was she?"

"The schoolmistress." Bryony shrugged. "There's always a schoolmistress."

There was one letter Tamsin had not laid out. It was folded smaller than the others, into quarters, and the paper was thinner and greyer, cheaper. The hand on the outer fold was upright and heavy, nothing like a clerk's: a hand that had been taught to write and had not much liked it. There was no date on the fold. When she opened it a crack she saw a short letter, five or six lines, signed only with an M. She closed it again and set it to one side, away from the rows, because it was not about the orchard and it was not Senna's, and because she had read enough for one afternoon to know she was not reading properly.

"I'd like to take them," she said. "To Calloway. For a week."

"No."

Tamsin waited. She had learnt at the Registry that most people who said no to a search request were saying it to the first sentence, and would answer the second one differently if you let a bit of time go past.

Bryony stood at the window with her mug and looked at her trees. "Orla's solicitor would have them off you in a day."

"Orla's solicitor will want to see them whatever happens, if I use them in the report. That's how it works. But nobody will have them off me. I'll write you a receipt for every one."

"A receipt."

"A list. Date, first line, number of sheets. You keep one, I keep one."

"Like a library."

"Like a library."

Bryony drank some of her tea. Outside, a blackbird went along the bottom of the hedge turning over leaves, and she watched it until it had finished.

"A week," she said.

"A week."

Tamsin wrote the list on two sheets from her notebook, thirty-four letters and one signed M., and Bryony read every line of her copy before she folded it into the pocket of her fleece. Then Bryony tied the tape round the bundle herself, with the knot in the old place, and put the walnut box back on the dresser. Something in it slid as she lifted it. She put her hand flat on the lid for a moment, the way she had held the letters, and then went to find a carrier bag that would not let the rain in.

The bus was at twenty past four. Bryony saw her to the gate and went back inside before Tamsin had reached the lane.
