The step is warmest at the left-hand end, where the wall of the porch takes the sun after midday, and Jory has had the left-hand end since the winter the old woman with the dog died. Before that she had the right. Before that she had the bridge, and before the bridge she had a room, but that was a long time ago and the room was not much.

In the mornings the step is cold whichever end you have. The stone holds the night like water. She sits up as soon as there is light, because the clerks start coming at seven and they do not like to step over someone lying down. Sitting, she is a person on a step. Lying, she is something else.

They come up the hill in ones and twos and go in by the side door, not this one, but they pass the porch to get there and some of them look in. Most of them do not. There is one with a basket who always says good morning and never stops, and one who drops a coin on the step without bending, the way you drop a coin in a well, and one young one who looks at her every day as if she were a sum that will not come out. Jory has stopped minding any of them. You cannot mind everybody. It is too much work.

She has the brown cloth round her shoulders and the blue one over her knees. The brown one was given to her in the winter. It is lengths of something that went wrong in the dyeing, stitched together down the long sides by somebody who knew how to stitch, and it is warmer than it looks, and when it rains it smells of roots.

At nine the bell goes for the morning reading and the chapel fills up, and the people going in have to step past her, and they do, with their skirts held to one side. At ten it empties. In between she listens to them read the names through the open door. She likes that part. She does not know any of the names, but she likes the way they are said, one after another, each one put down carefully like a cup on a shelf, and the answer coming back from the whole room at once, *kept*, like a door shutting softly a long way off.

Nobody has ever read her name. She was born on a boat on the river, on the way to somewhere, and boats were not written down then and are not now, so there is no roll anywhere with Jory in it. She thought about it more when she was younger. Now she thinks about it the way she thinks about her bad leg, which is to say when it hurts, which is not often, and mostly in the wet.

The cough is worse this week. It was worse last week too.

At midday the chapel woman brings the alms. It is bread today, and she puts it in Jory's hands and writes in her book, *Jory of the porch*, and what she has given, as she does every week, and Jory watches her write it with interest, because it is the only place her name goes. The chapel woman has a hand like a hedge. She writes very fast and goes away.

The soup comes in the afternoon.

It is not every day. It has never been every day, for a year or more, and Jory has never once known which days, so she does not wait for it, or she tells herself she does not. Today it comes late, after four, when the sun is off the step and the stone is cooling. It comes from the side door, not the front, the way the clerks go, and it comes in a brown jug with a cloth over it and a bowl in the other hand.

The registrar sits down on the step beside her without asking, as she always does, and pours, and holds the bowl until Jory has both hands round it.

"It's leek," she says. "There wasn't much."

"It's hot."

"That's the main thing."

She has ink on her fingers. She always has, and Jory has stopped seeing it, but today there is more than usual, right up the side of the hand and into the cuff, as if she has been writing all day and all of last night as well. She sits with her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging, and looks out at the hill and the roofs and the river, and does not say anything else, which is what she usually does. Some days she says a thing about the weather or the price of something. Some days she asks how the leg is. She has never asked Jory's name. She knew it the first day. She said it, *Jory*, as if it were written down somewhere and she had read it.

Jory eats the soup slowly because it is hot and because it is soup. A woman comes out of the chapel and goes down the steps and looks at the two of them sitting there, the registrar in her good dark coat and Jory in her blankets, and looks away quickly, as if she has seen something private.

"You're tired," Jory says.

"Everyone's tired. It's the end of the winter."

"It's the spring."

"Is it." The registrar looks at the hill, where there is a thin green on everything that was not there last week. "So it is."

When the bowl is empty she takes it and wipes it out with the cloth and puts it inside the jug. She does not get up straight away. She sits on for a little, longer than she usually does, with the jug between her feet, and Jory does not mind, because the step is getting cold and two on a step is warmer than one.

"I won't be up for a few days," the registrar says.

"All right."

"There's a thing at the Registry."

"All right," Jory says again, because there is always a thing at the Registry, and the soup is not every day, and has never been every day, and she has not once known which days.

The registrar stands up and picks up the jug. She looks down at Jory for a moment as if she is going to say something else, and then she does not, and she goes back along the front of the chapel to the side door the clerks use, with her inky hand round the handle of the jug, and goes in.

Jory pulls the brown cloth up round her ears. It smells of roots. Inside, through the open door, they are lighting the lamps for the evening reading, and soon they will start on the names.
