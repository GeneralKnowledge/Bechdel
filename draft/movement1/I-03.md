The public way into the search room was round the side of the Registry, through a door with a ramp and an intercom and a laminated sign about bags. The quick way was through the chapel. Tamsin had used the quick way every working morning for fifteen years, and when she came up the hill on Thursday with her freelancer's card in one pocket and her old staff pass in the other, her feet took her to the chapel door before she had thought about it, and she let them.

It was nine o'clock and the chapel was empty except for a woman with a mop at the far end and the smell of the radiators, which came on at eight and smelt of burnt dust until ten. The light was grey through the tall windows. Down the north aisle, under their low lamps, were the cases.

She did not look at them. That was how you walked past them when you worked there: the way you walked past a clock in your own kitchen, knowing what it said without reading it. Five long cases of dark wood and glass, each with a roll open in it at a page that had been chosen two centuries ago and turned once a year since, on Senna's Day, by the Registrar-General in gloves. The ink on the rolls was brown now, even, a single tone from end to end. In the case nearest the door, where the light was best, there was a page with four corrections in its margin, and each correction had the stroke at the foot of it, the little hook turning back. Tamsin saw it out of the side of her eye the way she always had and kept walking.

Between the second case and the third there was a smaller one, newer, that the Order had put in for the anniversary with a printed card: *Chapel alms book, 640s. Charity given at the chapel door.* The book lay open at a page of short entries in a cramped hand. *To the river women, bread. Jory of the porch, bread and a blanket. For candles, the vigil of Hild. To the river women, bread.* A tourist would read three lines and move on to the rolls. Tamsin read one and moved on to the door at the end of the aisle, the one marked PRIVATE, and put her old pass against the reader, and heard it click.

She stood for a second with her hand on the bar. Then she pushed it.

The corridor beyond was the same as ever: cream paint to waist height, green above it, noticeboards, a fire extinguisher with a festive bow on it that somebody had put there in 1010 and nobody had ever taken off. She went down it and through the swing doors at the end into the search room from the inside, as if she had come from the staff kitchen, and nobody looked up.

The search room was long and quiet and too warm. Six readers at the tables, two clerks at the desk. Tamsin signed the book at the desk, which made her legitimate, and asked for the Wexham eastern rolls, current series, and the plan for Pell Lane.

The clerk who brought them was someone she did not know, a young woman with a nose stud and chipped green varnish on her nails, who put the volume down with exaggerated care and said, "That's the facsimile, the original's in the chapel. You'll know that."

"I'll know that."

"Only people forget." The clerk went away.

Tamsin had not forgotten, exactly. She had stopped thinking about it, which was different. Every title in the eastern parishes, Wexham and its smallholdings and four villages besides, was searched from a facsimile of a roll that lay in a case in the chapel, because the roll in the case was the roll of record. It was Senna's. That was the point of it: the saint's own copy, saved from the door, made original by the grace of the Keeper in 649 and never since replaced. Half of Wexham's mortgages rested on it. People bought houses on it without knowing it existed.

She found the Rook parcels on the third page of the Pell Lane section. The entry was in the same brown, even ink as every page of the volume, reproduced in photographic grey. *The lower field of the Rook holding, from the brook to the middle of the pear row, being half the trees.* And in the margin beside *middle*, small, a word struck through, the replacement written after it, and the stroke at the foot, the little hook.

She sat back.

It meant very little. Senna had corrected hundreds of entries; you could not open the eastern rolls without finding one. But it was a correction, and it was on her line, and Tamsin's whole working life had been the business of asking what a correction was correcting. She tilted the facsimile towards the lamp and tried to read the word under the strike. A clerk's strike was meant to cancel, not to hide, and in the original you might have made something out. In a photograph of a photograph, there was nothing but a grey bar.

She wrote it down. *Rook lower field: line at middle of row. Correction in margin, S.A. stroke. Struck word illegible in facs. Check original?* Then she crossed out *original?*, because to check the original she would have to ask Agnes Mure, and wrote *other evidence* instead.

She worked through until half past eleven. The older plans gave her nothing new. The Rook holding had been two parcels for as long as there were plans of it, and the line had always run down the middle of the row, on paper. Whatever Orla's ditch was and whatever Bryony's stone was, they were older than the paper or had nothing to do with it.

At half past eleven she went out to the corridor to find the water fountain, and Agnes Mure was standing by the fire extinguisher with the bow on it, reading a noticeboard she had certainly read before.

"Tamsin."

"Agnes."

The Registrar-General was a tall woman, grey-haired, in a dark suit that was better than the ones she had worn when Tamsin worked for her. She had new glasses, rimless, which made her look more patient than she was. She had always looked as if she were waiting for something to be finished.

"I saw your name in the book."

"I'm doing a boundary in Wexham. Two sisters."

"I know. Gilmour's rang to check you were still in good standing." Agnes let that sit. "I told them you were."

"Thank you."

"I didn't do it for you." Agnes took her glasses off and looked at them, and put them back on. "You'll have noticed the building. The cases, the cards, the tea towels. We're three months off the anniversary. I've got the Order in and out of the chapel every day, the *Courier* ringing about the relics, the Copy Programme two years late, and a chapter vote on whether my rolls can be photographed by a television company. I don't need anybody making work."

"I'm doing a boundary."

"Then do a boundary." Agnes's voice did not change. It never had. It was the voice she used for dictating letters about fees. "Your access to this room is a courtesy, Tamsin. Freelancers come in through the side door, they sign the book, they search what they're paid to search, and they go home. In the anniversary year I'm not extending any courtesy to anybody who goes looking for questions in the eastern rolls. Not to you, not to Fen Archer, not to the Keeper herself."

"Nobody's looking for questions."

"Good." Agnes looked down the corridor towards the door marked PRIVATE, the chapel door, and then back at Tamsin, and Tamsin made herself not put a hand to the pocket where the old pass was. "Then we won't have to talk about it again."

She went off down the corridor with the slightly rolling walk Tamsin remembered from a thousand mornings, towards her office, towards the Copy Programme and the chapter vote and the television company, and did not look back.

Tamsin drank some water. It tasted of the pipes, as it always had. She thought, with a heat that surprised her, that Agnes had waited two years to say *courtesy* to her in that tone, and had probably enjoyed it, and that nothing Tamsin had done had earned it, and that this was what Agnes was like and always had been: small, careful, frightened of anything that was not a form.

She went back into the search room and returned the volume to the desk, and the clerk with green varnish said, "Find what you wanted?" and Tamsin said, "Some of it," and signed out.

She left by the side door with the ramp and the laminated sign about bags, out into the cold. It was only on the bus down the hill that she found she had walked round the outside of the building to avoid the chapel, and that her hand was in her pocket, on the pass.
