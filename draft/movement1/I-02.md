The piano was in the front room because there was nowhere else for it, and in the evenings the house sounded like a practice room with a kitchen attached. Tamsin heard it from the end of Linnet Street: the same four bars, a pause, the same four bars with one note changed, a pause, a chord struck flat-handed in irritation. She let herself in and stood in the hall in her wet coat and listened to it go round twice more before she called out.

"In here," Juno said. "Don't talk. Listen to this."

She was sitting sideways on the piano stool with one foot tucked under her, still in the cardigan she taught in, with a pencil in her mouth and another behind her ear. The score on the stand was covered in her handwriting, crossings-out on crossings-out, the staves so dense with pencil in places that they had gone grey. The music had started life in September as twelve neat pages and was now twenty-three, three of them taped to the edges of the others.

"I'm listening."

"It's the last verse. I've had the last verse wrong for a month." Juno took the pencil out of her mouth. "Everyone knows *Wallgate*, so whatever I do with the last line, they'll be singing *Wallgate* in their heads over the top of it. So I can't fight it. I've got to go under it. Here."

She played the opening, and Tamsin, who had sung the hymn at every Naming Day since she was old enough to stand, felt the old tune start in her chest before she recognised that Juno had kept its first four notes and then gone somewhere else. It was lower than *Wallgate* and slower, and it moved by small steps where the old tune jumped, and when it came to the third line it hung on a chord that did not resolve.

Juno sang the words under her breath, not performing them, just to place them.

"*Write her where the fire is kept, read her when the house is still; though the hand that wrote her fail.*" She stopped on the held chord and looked up. "And then."

"And then."

"And then it's *another hand will*, and in *Wallgate* it goes up, everybody goes up, the altos shout. I'm having it come down. *Another hand will.* Very quiet. And then *another will*, and the sopranos take it over from the altos, so it's literally another hand. Do you see? It's passed on." She played it. It came down, very quiet, and the second phrase lifted out of the first like something being handed across a table. "Is that too clever?"

"No."

"Is it too quiet? Honor says the chapel eats anything below mezzo."

"It's lovely," Tamsin said, and meant it, and saw Juno hear that she meant it and then decide not to believe her because she had said it too fast.

"You always say that."

"It's always true."

Juno made a noise and turned back to the stand and wrote something in the margin very small. Tamsin took off her coat. On the kitchen table, among the post, the *Courier* was folded open at Fen Archer's new column, which had a drawing of a quill at the top of it and the heading **Senna for our times**, and underneath that, in smaller letters, *Every Saturday until midsummer: what the saint who died for the rolls can teach a city that has forgotten how to keep anything.*

She read it standing up, with her glasses pushed up on her forehead, holding it at arm's length.

*Three hundred and seventy-five years ago this spring, a young clerk of twenty-nine stood alone in the Registry of Calloway while a mob broke down the door. She could have run. She stayed, and passed the rolls out to safety one by one, until the last. The rolls she saved hang in the Registry Chapel to this day, and the city that grew up around them has never quite deserved them. Keeper Carrow, who had trained her, is said to have wept at her funeral. This year, as the Order finally moves to make her a saint, we might ask what Senna Aldous would make of a Registry that takes eleven weeks to answer a letter.*

Tamsin took a pencil from the jar on the windowsill and wrote *31* in the margin beside *twenty-nine*. Then she wrote *source?* beside the funeral, and then, because it was there, she underlined *eleven weeks*, which was unfair to the Registry but was also, as far as she knew, correct.

"Don't," Juno said, from the other room. "Fen's not going to read your corrections."

"Somebody might."

"Nobody reads the paper with a pencil except you and Agnes Mure, and you two would be the last people in Calloway to admit you had anything in common."

That was true, and Tamsin put the pencil back. She put the kettle on. Through the doorway she could see Juno's back, the stoop of her shoulders over the keys, the pencil going behind her ear and coming out again.

"How was Wexham?" Juno called.

"Muddy. Two sisters. They talk to each other through me."

"Like Kate's aunts."

"Worse. Kate's aunts at least look at each other." She found two mugs. "It's eight days. Maybe ten. It pays nearer four thousand."

"That's good."

"It's all right."

It was the third time that month she had said a job paid more than it did. She did not decide to do it. It came out the way a number comes out when someone asks your age and you are tired: a version you have practised. The difference was going to have to come from somewhere, eventually, and she had a vague idea that it would come from the next job, which would be better, and which she had not yet been offered.

Juno stopped playing. She came and leaned in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded and the pencil still behind her ear, and Tamsin knew what was coming from the way she leaned, the way she knew a document's age from its fold before she opened it.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can always ask me something."

"Are we going to have a child?"

Tamsin poured the water. She did it carefully, as if the kettle were heavier than it was. They had talked about it in the spring, properly, at this table, with a bottle of wine and a sheet of paper on which Juno had written two columns that had not, in the end, helped. They had talked about it again in August, on holiday, less properly. Since then Juno had asked twice, lightly, the way she asked whether Tamsin had remembered to put the bins out.

"Yes," Tamsin said. "I think so. After this job."

"After this job."

"It's eight days. Then it's the new year, and I'll have the fee, and I'll be able to think."

Juno looked at her. Then she looked at the newspaper on the table, at the pencil marks in the margin of Fen's column, the *31* and the *source?*, and something in her face settled, the way a person settles into a chair she has decided she is going to be sitting in for a while.

"All right," Juno said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do." She took her tea. "You always mean it."

She went back to the piano. Tamsin heard her sit, and heard the four bars of *Wallgate* that were not *Wallgate* begin again, and the long chord on the third line, and then *another hand will*, very quiet, coming down, and the sopranos' line lifting out of it. Juno played it through twice, without stopping and without crossing anything out, and then she shut the lid.

"I've got marking," she said. "Twenty-eight compositions on the theme of autumn. One of them is just the word *leaves* sung to every note of the scale."

"Is it good?"

"It's the best one."

Tamsin laughed, and Juno did not, quite, and went upstairs with the marking under her arm. Tamsin sat at the kitchen table with her tea going cold and the Registry extract out of its sleeve, and looked at the line down the middle of the pear row for a long time, as if it might move if she watched it, and thought about two sisters who would not look at each other, and about nine hundred pounds.
