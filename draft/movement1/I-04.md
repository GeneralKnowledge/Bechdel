It had rained all afternoon and stopped at five, and by the time Bryony walked down Tanner's Row the sky had cleared from the west and the cold was coming in behind it. Frost by midnight. She thought about the young trees at the top of the orchard, which did not matter in December, and about the pear row, which did not matter either until April, and thought about them anyway, the way she always did when the sky went clear.

The meeting house was warm. That was the first thing anyone said about it, and the last thing anyone from Calloway said about it, usually in a tone of surprise, as if dissenters ought to be cold. It was a plain room with plain chairs and white walls and nothing on the walls at all, and three big radiators that the meeting had voted for in 1987 after a winter when two of the older members had got bronchitis in the same month.

Saturday evenings were for the readers. There were nine of them on the rota and five had come, and they sat in the front row with their coats over the backs of the chairs and took turns at the lectern to read to each other and to the dozen or so people who came on Saturdays because they liked it better than Sundays. Nobody preached on a Saturday. You read, and people listened, and afterwards someone said *thank you* and someone else told you that you had gone too fast in the middle.

Bryony was fourth. She went up with the reading copy, which was a printed book now, small and green, *The Schoolmistress's Letters*, with the meeting house's own old manuscript copy kept in a cupboard and brought out only at Return Day. She found the page. She did not need it.

"*The Hollis child,*" she read, "*has made her capitals backwards for a month and I have decided to let her keep them.*"

She did not perform it. She had never been able to, and the meeting did not like it when people did. She read it the way she would have told someone where the gate was. *She makes them backwards perfectly. Every R faces the door.* There was a small laugh at that from the back, from the old woman by the radiator who laughed at it every time. *I asked her why, and she said so that they can see who is coming.*

Bryony liked this one best of all of them, and had never said so, because it was the one everybody liked. She liked the child. She liked the mother, who went away comforted or offended, *with her it is the same face*. She liked that the letter got to the Keeper in the middle and did not make a fuss about it: *I am not sure I believe the Keeper reads. I am sure that you and I can, and the child's mother, and the child. Perhaps that is enough to be going on with.* The room was quiet for that. It always was.

She read the stove and the flue and the plums. *Ada has eaten most of them and says the wasps did it.* She read *We are managing, which is to say the bread is bad*, and closed the book, and went and sat down.

"Thank you, Bryony," said the clerk of the meeting.

"You went too fast at the plums," said the reader next to her, who was eighty-four.

"I always go too fast at the plums."

"I know. It's because you like it."

Bryony thought about that while the fifth reader went up. It was possible.

She walked home the long way, up the high street and along Pell Lane in the dark, and the sky had cleared all the way over and the stars were out, and the puddles at the edges of the lane already had a skin on them. At the top of the lane she looked, without meaning to, at the place on the verge where Orla parked when she came, which she had not done for a year and four months except for Tuesday, with the woman from Calloway. The verge was empty. There were tyre marks in it from Tuesday that had filled with water and were freezing.

In the kitchen she put the kettle on and stood by the stove with her coat still on while it boiled, and looked at the dresser.

The box lived on the top shelf, behind the good plates that nobody used. It was a walnut sewing box with a split lid that someone had mended with brass tacks before anybody now alive was born. Their mother had kept it there, and their grandmother before that on a different dresser in the same kitchen. When they were girls, on wet afternoons, their mother had sometimes taken it down and let them look, and read them a bit, and Orla had always got bored first.

Bryony took down the good plates, two at a time, and put them on the table. Then she lifted the box down and opened it.

The letters were where they always were, tied in green tape that had gone the colour of string. She did not untie them. She knew roughly what was in them, the way you know what is in a drawer you never open. Underneath, at the bottom of the box, was a second packet, smaller, tied with brown tape in a hard knot, and on top of the knot a slip of paper in her mother's handwriting that said *Not to be read out*. Her mother had never said why. Bryony had asked once, at twelve, and her mother had said, *Because some things are for the family and some things are for the meeting, and that's for neither*, which had seemed at twelve to be an answer.

She left the brown packet where it was.

She untied the green tape and turned the letters over until she found the one she wanted, which she knew by its fold. She had read it to Tamsin Hale over the phone on Wednesday night, the part that mattered, and Tamsin had gone quiet on the other end and then asked if she could see it. *The pear row with it, to its eastern edge, as your mother worked it.* There it was. *Your* mother. Lise's mother, it meant, whoever she had been, a Rook in this kitchen three hundred and fifty years ago, working the row to its eastern edge.

She went through the rest more slowly. Lise and Lise and Lise, and a child with chilblains, and Ada and the plums, and there it was, the Hollis child, with the R facing the door. It was the one she had read three hours ago in the meeting house out of a green printed book, and here it was in a hand, with a word crossed out in the margin and a little hooked mark beside it, and the words were in the same order.

She stood and looked at it. Then she folded it up again and put it with the others.

The kettle had boiled and switched itself off. She made tea and sat at the table among the good plates with the letters in front of her.

Orla's solicitor would want them. Gilmour's would write a letter on thick paper asking for *any documents in your possession relevant to the boundary*, and Bryony would have to send them, or copies of them, to an office on the high street where Orla's friend's daughter worked on reception and told everyone everything. And then Orla would have them, or her builder would, or the builder's own solicitor in Calloway, and they would be in a file with a number on it. That was how it would go if she did nothing.

If she gave them to the woman from the Registry first, the woman from the Registry would use the one about the pear row and give the rest back. That was her job. She had a quiet way of standing in an orchard and looking at stones, and she did not talk about herself, and she was from Calloway, so she did not know anybody in Wexham to tell. It would stay between the two of them, and the family's business would stay in the family, near enough.

Bryony drank her tea and decided that, about halfway down the mug, the way she decided most things.

She tied the green tape round the letters again, with the knot in the old place. She put the brown packet back at the bottom of the box, under them, where it had always been. Then she put the box on the table by the door where she would see it in the morning, and put the good plates back on the dresser, two at a time, and went out with a torch to check the young trees at the top, though it was December, and nothing could happen to them.
